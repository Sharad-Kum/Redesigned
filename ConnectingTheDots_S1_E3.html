8 Questions, Not 6.

A checklist doesn't get better by getting longer. It gets better by getting harder to dodge.

Ed2 was about what happens when one leader holds more than one hat without letting one blur into the next. What actually keeps a hat from blurring is whether each hat is still forced to answer its own questions before it acts, regardless of who's asking.

The same principle applies to ideas, vision and strategy.

The structured problem-solving, planning, analysis and decision-making W&H method — in its original six-question form, known as the Kipling method — is not simply a checklist. It asks questions to eliminate ambiguity before ambiguity becomes a problem. It is a discipline for forcing clarity before an idea becomes a vision, and before a vision becomes a strategy.

The eight questions
Why: defines purpose.
What: fixes the destination.
Who: establishes ownership.
Whom: makes clear who the outcome is for.
Which: forces the alternatives and the choice into the open.
When: creates commitment.
Where: sets the context in which it must work.
How: turns intent into execution.

The move from 6 (5W1H) to 8 (7W1H) — Whom and Which added — is simply an example of how progressive, flexible and agile the methods and frameworks should remain.

What the questions do at each level
At the idea level, these questions crystallize whether the idea is worth advancing.
At the vision level, they replace abstraction with a precise picture of what is being created.
At the strategy level, they turn the vision into an execution-ready blueprint — one that aligns cross-functional teams around the same choices, priorities and actions.

Overlook or loosely address one of these, and ambiguity remains. Misalignment, conflicting interpretations, delayed decisions, or execution failure will appear downstream.

Because ambiguity doesn't disappear when a plan moves fast. It moves downstream — and eventually lands on someone else's desk as a decision they now have to make.

Not more questions. Better ones — harder to dodge.
